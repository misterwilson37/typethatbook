## What these are

Laura "Mother Wit" Belding and her friends at Central High in Centerport start a girls' athletic
league and then have to defend it — book 1 gets them a gymnasium, book 2 is rowing and canoeing,
book 3 is basketball. "Gertrude W. Morrison" is a Stratemeyer Syndicate house name, so these were
written to formula by an unnamed hand.

**They are unusually good for the period on one axis and ordinary on another.** The books are
sharply and consistently *anti-snob*: the villain of all three is a wealthy butcher's daughter, the
heroes include a grocer's daughter and a widow's daughter who works in a box factory, and book 1's
secret society picks a laborer's daughter over a rich girl who campaigned for it. There is real
argument in them about whether girls should be doing athletics at all, and the books are firmly on
the yes side.

The race writing is period-ordinary, and that is what most of the editing addressed.

### Hebron "Heb" Pocock

Book 3's villain is called `Hebe` sixty-three times, in the text and in two chapter titles. It's
short for Hebron and there's no antisemitism anywhere in the plot — but the word on the page is the
word on the page, and a sixth grader reads it sixty-three times. Renamed to `Heb`, which is the
natural shortening of the author's own `Hebron`.

⚠️ **One scene needed more than a rename.** In Chapter XVII a schoolmaster demands the boy's name
and then sneers *"You look like a 'Hebe.'"* The 1914 author knew what the word meant and built a
joke on it. That exchange is cut; the scene still has the master subduing the bully and dismissing
him, which is the part that matters.

### Mammy Jinny, the Beldings' cook

She appears in all three books, written in heavy eye-dialect, and her scenes exist to deliver
malapropisms. She's affectionately drawn and the family plainly loves her.

**The approach taken: keep her, drop the marking.** Her dialect is rendered in plain English,
`Mammy` becomes `Jinny`, and the race labels (`their old black cook`, `the black woman`) are
removed. Two bits of physical business went too — `rolling her eyes` and `showing all her white
teeth`, which are stock minstrel imagery rather than description.

**Her jokes are all intact**, because they're genuinely funny and have nothing to do with her race:
she confuses tuberculosis with consumption, insists dictionary-makers shouldn't use words longer
than two "syllabubs," and complains that literary people "litters things all over the house." Her
colloquial grammar is kept as well — she doesn't suddenly speak like Laura.

### Rufus Doyle's disability, book 3

Eleven further edits replace the period vocabulary with plain modern language, settling on one
word — **`slow`** — and using it consistently. `the weak-minded boy` → `the boy`. `the half-witted
lad` → `the lad`. `that half-foolish boy` → `that slow boy`. `Bill isn't much smarter than silly
Rufe` → `than Rufe himself`.

The best of them is Chet's line. `perhaps he did it because he's not quite right in his head`
becomes `perhaps he did it because he didn't understand what he was doing` — which replaces a label
with the actual reason, and happens to be more accurate to the plot.

His introduction went from *"without Bill's modicum of sense. A glance at Rufe told the pitiful
story. As his Irish father had said, Rufe was 'an innocent'"* to *"though slower to take a thing
in. He had been slow since he was small, and always would be."* Same fact, no pity.

### The narrator's sneering at poor people

These books are *about* class — the whole plot of book 3 is a butcher's daughter with new money
being frozen out by the Hill families, and book 1's secret society picks a laborer's daughter over
a rich girl. **The characters' snobbery is left completely alone**, because it's the subject, and
the books consistently show the snobs to be wrong.

What's been cut is the dozen places where **the narrator joins in** — where the book itself, in its
own voice, tells you that poor people are stupid, dirty, or a predictable type. Nobody in the story
gets to answer that, so it just sits there.

The one that prompted this: a widow dreading the hospital for her sick baby, explained by the
narrator as *"for with people of this degree of intelligence, to take a patient to the hospital is
equal to signing his death warrant."* The clause is gone; her grief isn't.

Also gone: `the Doyles' humble domicile` → `little house`; `a throng of the idle children and women
of the neighborhood` → `children and women from the neighborhood`; `Of course, the usual officious
neighbor had to rush into the house` → `One of the neighbors rushed into the house`; `They were
dreadful looking creatures` (cut — Laura's fear of them stays, since they're throwing stones);
`the better class of farmers` → `the better-off farmers`; `the simple Mrs. Grimes` → `Mrs. Grimes`;
`her coarse face` → `her face`.

⚠️ **One joke was cut**, in book 2: Bobby's four-line anecdote about the family's new Irish maid
not knowing what macaroni is. It's the only place a hero laughs at a servant with no answer from
the book, and it needs the maid to be poor and foreign in order to be funny.

**Three similar jokes were kept**, because they don't: the illiterate school trustee who marks the
class wrong for spelling *Egypt* correctly (the target is his pomposity), the Bohemian boy who
defines a calf as "the child of a cow and the back of your leg" (the joke is on English, and the
teacher says he's "smart as a whip"), and Mrs. Grimes calling the Four Corners "a shiftless, useless
lot" — she grew up there, and she says so in the next breath.

### The publisher's advertising pages are gone

**5,888 words removed** from books 1 and 2 — the Grosset & Dunlap back-catalogue listing Tom Swift,
the Rover Boys, the Outdoor Girls, and thirty-odd Horatio Alger titles. It isn't the book, and in a
typing program it's four thousand words a student would have to type. Book 3 had none.

The Project Gutenberg licence text is untouched in all three.

## Things to know before putting these in front of a class

Nothing below was edited, but you shouldn't be surprised by any of it.

- **Otto Sitz, the Swiss farm boy, speaks in heavy stage-German** across all three books
  (`Dat don't worry me von bit`). His family are decent, hardworking people; his father rescues
  Laura in book 1 and his sister Eve becomes one of the best athletes in the school. The accent was
  kept as a fact about him.
- **Tony Allegretto, an Italian organ-grinder** (book 2), speaks in accented English. He's a
  working man whose trained monkey the heroes nearly take from him, and the book lets him be angry
  about it. The boys' name-calling was edited; his accent wasn't.
- **Rufus Doyle** (book 3) is a young man with an intellectual disability. The 1914 vocabulary
  around him — `half-witted`, `weak-minded`, `half-foolish`, `an innocent`, `silly Rufe` — has been
  updated (see below). **His disability itself was kept**, because the plot depends on it: he's
  manipulated into wrecking the gymnasium precisely because he can't see what he's being used for.
  So is the book's affection for him — he's devoted to his baby brother, his mother says he's
  "real bright about some things," and the doctor says he's "'way above the average" in some ways.
  ⚠️ **The villain's insults to him (`gooney`, `softy`) were left standing**, because they're there
  to show you what the villain is, and the book punishes him for it.
- **Book 3 includes a blood transfusion**, described in some clinical detail — the villain
  volunteers a pint of her own blood to save a poor widow's dying child, and it's the turning point
  of the book.
- **Book 3 opens with a girl slapping another girl in the face** during a locker-room argument.
- **The class conflict is blunt and constant.** Wealthy characters look down on poorer ones in
  almost every chapter, and are wrong every time. That's the books' actual subject and it's left
  intact.

## The remaining two books

*The Girls of Central High on the Stage; Or, The Play That Took the Prize* and *…on Track and
Field*. Both on Project Gutenberg, both the same cast.

⚠️ **Read *On the Stage* before scanning it.** A 1914 girls' book whose entire plot is mounting a
theatrical production is the likeliest place in this whole series for costume-ethnicity or minstrel
material, and none of it would show up in a word scan. The first three books contain no staged
entertainment at all, so nothing here predicts what's in the fourth.
